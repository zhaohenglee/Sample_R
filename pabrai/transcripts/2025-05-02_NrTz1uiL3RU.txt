Jane Liu: Yes, nice. You have championed the idea of a rigorous investment checklist.

Mohnish: Yes, the checklist is a very useful tool, and it has helped us in aviation a lot,

checklists by pilots before they take off and before they land. I had applied the 
same concept. The aviation checklist came about from examining failures. 
When a plane crashed, the FAA would always go in and try to figure out why 
the crash happened. Then the FAA takes a pragmatic view of reality. They have 
a definition of what a human life is worth. I have not kept up, but it used to be 
around $10 million a few years back. When they see a plane crash and they see

the cost of that?” They will only make those changes if the cost is less than 4 
billion. It is a very pragmatic approach because you do not want air travel to be 
so expensive that we cannot get on an airplane, and you do not want it to be 
planes crashing all the time, because that also does not work. They have chosen 
this middle path, which has worked well. I did the same thing when I developed 
the pre-investment checklist. I looked at the great minds of investing who had 
had investments they made that did not work, but the reason why the

Jane Liu: My next question is about staying within the circle. The circle of competence is 
a core principle in your investing style. How do you define that circle today, and 
what practices helped you stay within it, especially as new industries and 
trends emerge? 
Mohnish: The circle of competence is a wonderful tool that Graham, Buffett, and Munger 
have emphasized for a long time. There are a few interesting things about the 
circle of competence. The first is, if you ask the question, if something is within 
your circle of competence or not, you have already answered it. To ask the 
question is to answer it. The fact that you are questioning that is that there is 
something in my circle of competence means it is not in your circle of 
competence. The second issue with the circle of competence is that we do not 
pay much of a penalty if we have a very narrow circle of competence. People 
are always trying to broaden their circle of competence, but quite frankly, you 
could always have a narrow circle of competence and do extremely well. The 
most important part of the circle of competence is not the size of the circle; it 
is staying in the epicenter of the circle. If we look at most entrepreneurs, they 
have a very narrow circle of competence. They are an inch wide and a mile 
deep, and if they were not that way, they would not be able to build successful 
companies. Most entrepreneurs do not know a lot about many things, but they 
can do extremely well because they know a lot about what they are focused 
on, and that works out well. Staying within the circle, being in the center, it is a 
natural process in investing that over a lifetime, the circle will expand. If you are 
reading, thinking, and experiencing different things over time, you will 
understand more things about how the world works, and so the circle will 
expand. We do not need to have some deliberate endeavor to expand the 
circle. It will expand naturally. We also need to be very clear on saying “no” very 
quickly when something is outside a circle. It is a big exercise in humility. 
Buffett has this too-hard pile box on his desk. It is a physical box that says “too 
hard.” He says that 99% of stuff should go into it. Most things that we encounter 
from my investing perspective should go into the too-hard pile. They should be 
outside the circle of competence, and we should not be trying to figure 
everything out.

Jane Liu: The next question is about volatility as a long-term advantage. You said

have used the volatility as an opportunity, especially during the times of panic 
or uncertainty, and maybe the past few weeks? 
Mohnish: If we look at stocks as businesses that have an intrinsic value, whatever causes 
that value to go down is an advantage for us long term if it is within the circle 
of competence. Volatility is always our friend. The reason why we are all 
interested in auction-driven markets is that they will undershoot and 
overshoot. Some news comes out, and Coke will go down in price. Did their 
long-term cash flows go down from some particular news? When you try to 
overlay news events that are moving markets, and then you overlay them on a 
particular business, you may find that the correlation is very weak. When you 
find those weak correlations, that usually is a sign that there could be an 
opportunity there, in the sense that something is being mispriced, then. 
Jane Liu: The next question is about macro and the micros striking a balance. How do

For most companies, the internal decisions that they are making and their 
competitors are making are going to drive most of the outcome. Usually, it is

years from now just by looking at those factors; the internal and market forces 
around that business. If we start to try to overlay macro things on top of that, 
we are going to get lost for the most part, because I do not think it becomes 
very hard to handicap. We want to try to look at things where the thesis is very 
simple and well understood from an internal perspective.
